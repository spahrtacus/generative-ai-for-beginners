## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:35)

> **[VISUAL: Night. A cemetery. Workers lower a lead-lined coffin into a deep pit. Lantern light. Wet concrete pours from a chute.]**

In October 1897, one of the richest men in America was buried at night.

His family didn't trust the ground to hold him. They lined the coffin with lead, sank it into a pit eight feet deep, and poured tons of concrete on top. **[SOLID: 1897 NYT account via WTTW; steel-rail detail DISPUTED]**

> **[VISUAL: Slow push-in on the finished grave. Fog.]**

They weren't afraid of grave robbers. They were afraid of his own workers.

> **[VISUAL: Title card: "PULLMAN"]**

This is the story of how George Pullman built the most perfect town in America, and why the people who lived there hated him enough that his family buried him under concrete.

### ACT 1: THE MAN WHO SOLD SLEEP (0:35–2:30)

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of George Pullman, slow Ken Burns zoom.]**

George Pullman got rich selling a dream: sleeping on a train.

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1870s luxury sleeping car interior, velvet berths, brass lamps, a porter turning down a bed.]**

In the 1860s, rail travel was miserable: hard benches, soot and days without a bed. Pullman built rolling hotels with fold-down berths, carpet and fine wood paneling. Railroads didn't buy his cars. They leased them, and Pullman kept collecting money on every one, forever. **[SOLID]**

By the late 1870s, the Pullman Palace Car Company was one of the most profitable businesses in America.

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1870s Chicago, crowded tenements, smoke, labor protest crowds.]**

But Pullman had a problem. Chicago was full of strikes, saloons and labor organizers, and he believed the problem was the environment. Bad houses made bad workers.

So he decided to build a better one.

### ACT 2: THE PERFECT TOWN (2:30–5:00)

> **[VISUAL: AI aerial: prairie south of Chicago, 1880. Then a time-lapse of brick rows rising.]**

In 1880, the company bought about 4,000 acres south of Chicago and built a town from nothing. **[SOLID: NPS]** It was named, of course, Pullman.

> **[VISUAL: AI: tree-lined street, identical red-brick rowhouses, gas lamps, children playing, clean sidewalks.]**

It had brick homes with indoor plumbing and gas. That was a luxury most workers in America had never seen. It had parks, a lake, a school, a church, a theater and a library. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: the Arcade building, a glass-roofed indoor shopping hall with shops and a post office.]**

At its center stood the Arcade, a glass-covered indoor shopping center, decades before the shopping mall existed. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Map graphic (code-built): Pullman vs. downtown Chicago, population counter rising.]**

By 1893, about 12,000 people lived there. **[SOLID]** Newspapers called it the most perfect city in the world.

> **[VISUAL: Slow pan, then the camera stops on a "PROPERTY OF PULLMAN PALACE CAR CO." sign.]**

But there was a catch, and it was built into every brick.

The company owned the land, the houses, the stores and the church. It owned everything. **[SOLID]** You couldn't buy your home. You could only rent it from your employer.

> **[VISUAL: AI: a hotel bar, polished wood, empty except for a well-dressed traveler.]**

There were no saloons. Liquor was sold at the company's Hotel Florence, at prices most workers couldn't afford. **[SOURCED]**

And the whole town was designed as an investment. Rents were set to earn the company about a 6% return on what it cost to build. **[SOLID]**

The town wasn't a charity. It was a business that happened to have people living in it.

### ACT 3: THE MATH STOPS WORKING (5:00–7:15)

> **[VISUAL: Archival: 1893 newspaper headlines about the Panic of 1893, bank runs.]**

In 1893, the American economy collapsed. Banks failed, railroads went bankrupt, and orders for new sleeping cars dried up.

> **[VISUAL: Animated chart (code-built): wage line dropping ~25–30%; rent line flat.]**

Pullman responded the way a businessman would. He cut wages, in a series of reductions through 1893, by roughly a quarter. **[SOLID: ~25% is the common figure. One striker's statement says nearly 30%.]**

But he did not cut the rent. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: close-up of a pay envelope opened at a kitchen table, coins on the wood, a rent receipt beside it.]**

Rent came straight out of workers' paychecks, before they ever saw the money. **[SOURCED: Brecher]** Families who had worked a full two weeks opened their pay envelopes and found almost nothing left.

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Pullman, harsher crop.]**

Why not lower the rent? Because the town had promised its investors a return. Lowering rents would mean the town missed its numbers.

The house and the job had been two separate deals. When the company squeezed both at once, people had nowhere to go.

> **[VISUAL: AI: workers gathering outside the factory gate at dawn, breath visible, tense.]**

In May 1894, a committee of workers asked for the cuts to be reversed or the rents lowered. The company said no. **[SOLID]**

On May 11, 1894, nearly 4,000 Pullman workers walked off the job. **[SOLID]**

### ACT 4: THE COUNTRY STOPS (7:15–9:45)

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Eugene V. Debs.]**

The strikers had joined a new union: the American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs.

In late June, the union made a decision that turned a local strike into a national emergency. Its members would refuse to handle any train carrying a Pullman car. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Animated map (code-built): railroad lines going dark across the country, spreading west from Chicago.]**

Because Pullman cars were on almost every major passenger line, the boycott spread across the country. Trains stopped from Chicago to California. It became one of the largest labor actions in American history. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Grover Cleveland.]**

The railroads went to Washington. The boycott was blocking the U.S. mail, they argued, and that made it a federal problem.

> **[VISUAL: AI: soldiers stepping off a train into a Chicago rail yard, bayonets, smoke.]**

In July, President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops into Chicago. The governor of Illinois objected. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: night, rail cars burning, silhouettes, chaos. Keep it impressionistic, no gore.]**

Rail yards burned. Crowds clashed with soldiers. By the time it was over, as many as 30 people were dead. **[SOURCED: "as many as thirty". Keep the hedge.]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: a jail cell door closing. Then the Debs photo again.]**

The strike was broken. Debs was jailed for defying a federal court order. **[SOLID]** In prison he read socialist writing, and he later ran for president five times.

> **[VISUAL: Archival-style: a congressional document, a pen signing.]**

And one more thing happened that summer. In late June 1894, just as the boycott was spreading, Congress made Labor Day a national holiday. **[SOLID: signed June 28, 1894]** It's often described as a peace offering to workers, but historians still debate how much the strike had to do with it. **[DISPUTED: present it as "often described"]**

### ACT 5: THE TOWN ON TRIAL (9:45–11:15)

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1890s courtroom, wood paneling, lawyers in dark suits.]**

The strike was over, but Pullman's troubles weren't.

The State of Illinois sued the Pullman Company. Its argument: the company's charter allowed it to *build railroad cars*, not to own and run an entire town. **[SOLID]**

In 1898, the Illinois Supreme Court agreed. It ordered the company to sell off everything in the town that had nothing to do with manufacturing: the houses, the stores, all of it. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: "FOR SALE" signs on the brick rowhouses, a family standing on the steps.]**

By about 1909, the homes had been sold. **[SOLID: NPS]** The perfect company town no longer belonged to the company.

George Pullman didn't live to see it. He died in October 1897, the year before the ruling.

> **[VISUAL: Return to the cemetery set from the cold open, now in daylight.]**

So his family buried him at night, under concrete.

### CLOSE (11:15–12:00)

> **[VISUAL: Modern-day: the real Pullman neighborhood, rowhouses still standing, a National Monument sign.]**

The brick houses are still there. Today Pullman is a National Monument. **[SOLID: designated 2015]** People live in the same homes the company once refused to sell.

> **[VISUAL: Slow pull back over the neighborhood. Music swells.]**

George Pullman believed that if you controlled where people lived, you could control how they behaved. He was right about one thing: a house changes people.

He just never imagined it could change them into his enemies.

> **[VISUAL: End card: subscribe and next video.]**

*Next time: the coal camps where workers weren't paid in dollars at all, but in company money that was only good at the company store.*
