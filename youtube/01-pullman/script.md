## SCRIPT

### COLD OPEN (0:00–0:45)

> **[VISUAL: Night. A cemetery. Lanterns. Workers lower a lead-lined coffin into a deep pit.]**

In October 1897, one of the richest men in America was buried at night.

His family didn't trust the ground to hold him. They sealed his coffin in lead, lowered it into a pit eight feet deep, and poured concrete on top until it was a block of stone. **[SOLID: 1897 NYT account via WTTW. Steel-rail detail is DISPUTED, so leave it out.]**

> **[VISUAL: Wet concrete pours from a chute. Slow push-in.]**

They weren't worried about ordinary grave robbers. They were worried about the people who had worked for him.

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of George Pullman. Hold.]**

His name was George Pullman. He built the most famous model town in America, a place with clean brick houses, parks and a library, where every family had what most workers only dreamed of.

> **[VISUAL: Title card: "PULLMAN"]**

So how did the man who built the perfect town end up so hated that his family buried him under concrete?

### ACT 1: THE MAN WHO SOLD SLEEP (0:45–2:45)

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1850s Chicago, a brick building lifted on hundreds of jackscrews, workers at each one, the street muddy below.]**

Pullman made his first money in Chicago by lifting buildings. In the 1850s the city was raising its streets out of the swamp mud to install sewers, and every building had to come up with them. Pullman's crews put whole brick buildings on jackscrews and raised them, a few inches at a time, while business carried on inside. **[SOLID]**

That taught him an early lesson: if you solve a problem for a city, the city pays you well.

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1860s passenger car interior, hard wooden benches, soot, exhausted travelers.]**

In the 1860s, a long train trip in America was miserable. You sat upright on a hard bench for days, breathing coal smoke, with nowhere to sleep.

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1870s Pullman sleeping car interior, velvet berths, brass lamps, polished walnut.]**

George Pullman sold a different experience: a hotel on wheels. He made fold-down berths with clean sheets, carpet and polished wood. Passengers paid extra to ride in his cars. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Code-built graphic: a railroad map; Pullman cars light up across every line.]**

His real genius was the business model. Railroads usually didn't own the cars. Pullman's company kept them and staffed them, and collected money on every ride, on line after line across the country. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: a sleeping car porter in a crisp white jacket making up a berth, back to camera.]**

Every car had a porter, and Pullman hired Black men for the job, many of them formerly enslaved. Passengers often didn't bother learning their names. They called every porter "George," after the man who owned the company. **[SOURCED: several porter histories. Keep the phrasing to "often" / "commonly".]**

Remember the porters. They come back at the end of this story.

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1870s Chicago, crowded tenements, saloons, a labor rally under smoky skies.]**

By the late 1870s, Pullman was very rich, and very worried. Chicago was full of strikes, saloons and labor organizers. Pullman believed bad surroundings made bad workers, and that bad workers went on strike.

So he decided to build his workers a better world, one he owned completely.

### ACT 2: THE PERFECT TOWN (2:45–5:30)

> **[VISUAL: AI aerial: empty prairie south of Chicago, 1880. Time-lapse: streets, then brick rows rising.]**

In 1880, his company bought about 4,000 acres of prairie south of Chicago and started building a town from nothing. **[SOLID: NPS]**

He hired a young architect, Solon Beman, and a landscape designer, Nathan Barrett. Construction began in May 1880. The first residents moved in on New Year's Day, 1881. **[SOURCED: NPS / Pullman Museum]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: tree-lined street, red-brick rowhouses, gas lamps, children playing, clean sidewalks.]**

It looked nothing like the rest of industrial America. There were brick homes with gas and running water, paved streets, parks and a lake, a school, a church and a theater. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: the Arcade, a glass-roofed indoor shopping hall, shops below, library upstairs.]**

At the center stood the Arcade, a glass-covered building with shops and a post office downstairs and a library and theater upstairs. It was essentially an indoor shopping mall, decades before malls existed. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Code-built: map of Pullman next to Chicago; population counter 8,860 → 12,000.]**

People came. About 8,600 lived there by 1885 and about 12,000 by 1893. **[SOLID]** Visitors toured it like a world's fair exhibit. The press called it a model for the future.

> **[VISUAL: Slow pan down a perfect street; stop on a sign: "PROPERTY OF PULLMAN'S PALACE CAR CO."]**

But living in Pullman came with conditions.

The company owned the land, the houses, the stores, the church and the water. **[SOLID]** You couldn't buy your home. You could only rent it from your employer, and rent came out of your paycheck. **[SOURCED: Brecher]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: an empty, elegant hotel bar; a traveler in a fine suit; a worker glancing in from the street.]**

There were no saloons. Liquor was sold at the company's Hotel Florence, at prices workers couldn't afford. **[SOURCED]**

> **[VISUAL: Code-built graphic: a ledger page. "Cost to build: $X → Rent target: 6% return."]**

And the town was never a charity. Rents were set so the town would return about 6% a year on what it cost to build. **[SOLID]** The town was meant to make a profit.

> **[VISUAL: Code-built: side-by-side rent comparison cards.]**

The workers did the math themselves. Later they claimed that a home they rented for $15 a month in Pullman would cost about $8 in Roseland, the neighborhood next door. **[CLAIM: strikers' statement, June 1894]** And they said the company bought water from the city of Chicago at a low price and sold it to residents at a 500% markup. **[CLAIM: strikers' statement]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: dusk; a family on the stoop of a perfect brick house, quiet, looking at the factory smokestacks.]**

A federal commission would later describe the arrangement this way: the company held, quote, "its assumed right to fix wages and rents absolutely." **[SOURCED: 1894 U.S. Strike Commission report, as quoted by the Illinois State Museum]**

The company paid your wages and the company set your rent. As long as the economy was booming, that worked.

### ACT 3: THE MATH STOPS WORKING (5:30–8:00)

> **[VISUAL: Archival: 1893 newspaper headlines, bank runs, shuttered businesses.]**

Then came 1893. The Panic of 1893 was one of the worst depressions in American history. Banks failed, railroads went bankrupt, and orders for new railcars collapsed.

> **[VISUAL: Code-built animated chart: wage line stepping down five times through 1893; rent line flat.]**

Pullman did what many businessmen did. He cut wages, and then cut them again. By the end of 1893, pay had fallen by about a quarter. **[SOLID: ~25% is the common figure]** The strikers put the last cut at "nearly thirty percent." **[CLAIM: strikers' statement]**

But he did not cut the rent. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: close-up of a pay envelope opened on a kitchen table. A few coins. A rent receipt beside it.]**

Rent came out of the paycheck first, before the worker ever saw the money. A man could work two full weeks and open an envelope with almost nothing left to feed his family. **[SOURCED: Brecher, strikers' statement]**

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Pullman.]**

Why not cut the rent? Because the town had a target to hit. The company argued the rents were already too low to earn a fair return. **[SOURCED: company statement, Illinois State Museum]** In its view, the factory and the town were two separate businesses, and the town's numbers had to work on their own.

For the people living there, they weren't separate. The same company had cut their pay and was still collecting the same rent.

Outside the town, the depression was pushing rents down. The strikers said some landlords elsewhere in Chicago had cut rents by as much as half. **[CLAIM: strikers' statement]** Pullman rents didn't move.

> **[VISUAL: AI: a cramped kitchen at night; a mother counting coins, children asleep behind her.]**

So families in the most famous model town in America started going hungry. They were living in clean brick houses with gas and running water and couldn't afford to feed their kids.

> **[VISUAL: AI: a sewing room inside the shops, rows of women at machines, one young woman looking up.]**

One of the workers was Jennie Curtis, a young seamstress in the company's repair shops. She became president of the girls' union local at Pullman. **[SOLID: NPS]** She later testified that after her father died, the company expected her to pay off his back rent out of her own wages. **[SOURCED: Illinois State Museum "Voices: Jennie Curtis". Verify wording before the final cut.]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: workers gathered at the factory gate at dawn, breath visible.]**

In May 1894, a committee of workers asked the company to restore wages or lower rents. The company refused, and several members of the committee were laid off. **[SOLID]**

On May 11, 1894, Pullman workers walked off the job. Several thousand people stopped working at once, and the factory went quiet. **[SOLID: ~3,000–4,000. Say "several thousand".]**

### ACT 4: THE COUNTRY STOPS (8:00–10:30)

> **[VISUAL: AI: the silent factory floor, half-built sleeping cars, dust in the light from high windows.]**

At this point it was still one company and one town on the edge of Chicago. Within weeks it would shut down trains across the country, bring the U.S. Army into Chicago and end up at the Supreme Court.

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Eugene V. Debs.]**

The strikers had joined a new union: the American Railway Union, led by Eugene V. Debs.

The union admitted white railroad workers only. The Black porters who worked in the same Pullman cars were shut out. **[SOURCED]**

At its June convention, the union voted on a plan. Its members would refuse to handle any train carrying a Pullman car. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Code-built animated map: rail lines going dark, spreading from Chicago west to California.]**

Because Pullman cars ran on almost every major line, the boycott spread fast. By the end of June, about 125,000 workers on 29 railroads had joined. **[SOURCED: Britannica]** Trains stopped across much of the country.

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of President Grover Cleveland.]**

The railroads went to Washington. They argued that the boycott was blocking the U.S. mail, which made it a federal crime.

On July 2, a federal court ordered the union to stop. **[SOLID]** Days later, President Grover Cleveland sent federal troops into Chicago, over the objections of the governor of Illinois. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: soldiers stepping off a train into a smoky rail yard. Then: night, rail cars burning, impressionistic, no gore.]**

Rail yards burned. Crowds clashed with soldiers. By the time it was over, as many as 30 people were dead. **[SOURCED: "as many as thirty". Keep the hedge.]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: a jail door closing. Debs photo.]**

The strike collapsed. Debs was convicted of contempt of court for defying the order and sentenced to six months in the jail at Woodstock, Illinois. **[SOLID]** He appealed. In 1895, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled against him unanimously. **[SOLID: In re Debs, 9–0]** That decision gave companies a powerful legal weapon against strikes for decades.

Debs came out of jail a different man, and went on to run for president five times. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: Archival-style: a congressional bill, a pen signing.]**

And in late June 1894, just as the boycott was spreading, Congress made Labor Day a national holiday. **[SOLID: signed June 28, 1894]** It's often described as a gesture to calm workers, though historians debate how much the strike had to do with it. **[DISPUTED: keep "often described"]**

### ACT 5: THE TOWN ON TRIAL (10:30–11:20)

> **[VISUAL: AI: 1890s courtroom, dark wood, lawyers in black suits.]**

Pullman had won the strike, but his troubles weren't over.

The State of Illinois sued the Pullman Company, arguing its charter allowed it to *build railroad cars*, not to own and run an entire town. In 1898, the Illinois Supreme Court agreed and ordered the company to sell everything it owned that had nothing to do with manufacturing. **[SOLID]**

> **[VISUAL: AI: "FOR SALE" signs on the rowhouses; a family on the steps.]**

By about 1909, the houses had been sold off. **[SOLID: NPS]** The perfect company town no longer belonged to the company.

George Pullman didn't see it happen. He died in October 1897, the year before the ruling, and his family buried him under concrete.

### THE TWIST AND CLOSE (11:20–12:15)

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of Robert Todd Lincoln.]**

The man who took over the Pullman Company was Robert Todd Lincoln, the son of Abraham Lincoln. **[SOLID]** He kept the company's hiring practices for the porters, the men passengers still called "George." **[SOURCED: Tye, "Rising from the Rails", via review]**

> **[VISUAL: Archival photo of A. Philip Randolph.]**

In 1925, the porters organized. Led by A. Philip Randolph, they formed the Brotherhood of Sleeping Car Porters, the first major Black-led union in America. **[SOLID]** In 1937, after a 12-year fight, the Pullman Company signed a contract with them. **[SOLID]**

The workers George Pullman had treated as invisible won the fight that his own white workers had lost in 1894.

> **[VISUAL: Modern-day: the Pullman neighborhood today, rowhouses still standing; National Monument sign.]**

The brick houses still stand. Today Pullman is a national park. **[SOLID: monument 2015, national historical park 2023]** Families own the homes the company once refused to sell.

> **[VISUAL: Slow pull back over the rooftops. Music swells.]**

George Pullman believed that if you controlled where people lived, you could control how they behaved. He built a town to keep his workers quiet, and that town became the place where they organized against him.

> **[VISUAL: Montage: modern corporate campuses, cranes over new employee housing (generic, no logos).]**

The idea didn't die with him. Over the next century, mill towns, mining towns and factory towns used the same arrangement, with the same company owning the jobs and the homes. Today, some of the biggest companies in the world are building housing for their own employees again.

The houses look better now. Whether the deal is any better is something those employees will find out the next time the economy turns.

> **[VISUAL: End card.]**

*Next time: the coal camps where workers weren't paid in dollars at all, but in company money that was only good at the company store.*
